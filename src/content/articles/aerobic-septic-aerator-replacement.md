An aerobic septic system's aerator (also called a blower) typically lasts 3 to 5 years before it needs replacement, and the swap costs $200 to $600 for the unit alone or $300 to $900 with professional installation. The aerator is a separate component from the system's effluent pump; it injects oxygen into the treatment tank to support the bacteria that give an aerobic treatment unit (ATU) its higher level of wastewater treatment, and it's the part most likely to fail first on this type of system.

If your home has an aerobic treatment unit rather than a conventional septic tank, you agreed to more mechanical maintenance in exchange for a system that works on smaller lots or poorer soil than a conventional field could handle. The aerator is where most of that ongoing maintenance actually shows up.

## What does the aerator actually do?

An ATU injects oxygen into the treatment tank, which supports aerobic bacteria that break down waste faster and more thoroughly than the anaerobic bacteria in a conventional septic tank, similar in principle to a small-scale municipal treatment plant ([EPA, Types of Septic Systems](https://www.epa.gov/septic/types-septic-systems)). The aerator, a small compressor or blower unit usually mounted at or near the tank, is what supplies that oxygen continuously. Without it running, the tank reverts to anaerobic conditions and loses the added treatment level the whole system was designed around.

## What are the signs an aerator is failing?

An unusually quiet tank is often the first sign, since a running aerator produces a constant, low hum that most homeowners tune out until it stops. Beyond silence, watch for a sewage odor stronger than normal (aerobic treatment reduces odor compared to anaerobic breakdown, so its absence is noticeable), an alarm on the control panel if your system has one, or a compliance notice if your state requires an annual service contract inspection and the inspector flags reduced aeration. Since most ATUs run on a 3-to-5-year aerator lifespan, age alone is worth checking even without obvious symptoms.

## How much does aerator replacement cost?

| Scope | Typical cost (2026) |
|---|---|
| Aerator/blower unit only (DIY-comfortable swap) | $200 to $600 |
| Professional installation, parts and labor | $300 to $900 |
| Full replacement including diagnostics and any related repair | $500 to $2,000 |

Figures aggregated from 2026 aerobic septic system cost data across multiple industry sources, consistently reporting the aerator as the most common recurring maintenance expense on an ATU, expected roughly every 3 to 5 years. The wider $500 to $2,000 range reflects jobs where the aerator failure is caught alongside another issue, such as a failing diffuser or control panel problem, rather than a straightforward like-for-like swap.

## Is this a DIY-friendly repair or does it need a professional?

Many ATU manufacturers design the aerator/blower as a swappable unit specifically because it's a wear item, so a comfortable DIYer can sometimes handle a straightforward replacement with the correct model. That said, most states with mandatory ATU service contracts require a licensed provider to perform maintenance and repairs as a condition of the permit, which makes this one of the few septic repairs where "can I do it myself" and "am I allowed to" are separate questions worth checking before you buy a part.

## Does a service contract already cover aerator replacement?

Sometimes, and it's worth checking your specific contract terms rather than assuming either way. Most states require ATUs to carry an ongoing service contract as a permit condition, and some contracts bundle routine parts replacement, including the aerator, into the annual fee, while others charge for parts and labor separately from the inspection visit. If you're buying a home with an ATU, confirm whether the existing service contract is current and transferable, since a lapsed contract is a compliance issue that follows the property.

## How does this differ from a regular septic pump?

The aerator supplies air, not wastewater movement; it never touches liquid directly, unlike an effluent or grinder pump that physically moves wastewater to the drain field. Some ATUs also include a separate effluent pump to dose treated water out to the field, meaning a single aerobic system can have two entirely different mechanical parts on two different failure and replacement schedules, which is worth keeping straight when a technician diagnoses a problem.

## FAQ

**How often does an aerobic septic system's aerator need replacing?**
Most aerators last 3 to 5 years before replacement, making it the most common recurring maintenance cost on an aerobic treatment unit. Age alone is worth checking even without obvious symptoms, since a gradually failing aerator doesn't always announce itself clearly.

**How much does it cost to replace an aerator on an aerobic septic system?**
The unit alone costs $200 to $600; professional installation with labor runs $300 to $900. A replacement that turns up an additional issue, like a failing diffuser or control panel problem, can run $500 to $2,000 total.

**How do I know if my aerator has failed?**
A tank that's gone unusually quiet (the aerator normally produces a constant low hum), a stronger-than-normal sewage odor, or an alarm on the control panel are the clearest signs. Age is also a factor, since most aerators are due for replacement within 3 to 5 years regardless of symptoms.

**Can I replace my own ATU aerator?**
The physical swap is often DIY-feasible with the correct replacement unit, but many states require a licensed service provider to perform ATU maintenance as a condition of the mandatory service contract. Check your state's specific requirement before assuming DIY replacement is allowed on your system.

**Is the aerator the same thing as the septic pump?**
No. The aerator injects air into the tank to support aerobic bacteria and never touches wastewater directly. An effluent or grinder pump physically moves wastewater to the drain field. Some aerobic systems have both components, each on its own separate failure and replacement schedule.

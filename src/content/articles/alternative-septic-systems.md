Alternative septic systems, including mound systems, aerobic treatment units, sand filters, and drip distribution, are used when a lot's soil depth, groundwater level, slope, or size rules out a conventional gravel drain field. They typically cost $7,000 to $20,000 or more installed, roughly double to triple a conventional system's $3,000 to $7,000, because they add pumps, sand media, or engineered treatment stages a standard system doesn't need.

You don't choose an alternative system by preference. A licensed site evaluator determines whether your lot's soil, groundwater depth, and slope can support a conventional drain field, and if it can't, the evaluator specifies which alternative fits the specific limitation, according to the [EPA's overview of septic system types](https://www.epa.gov/septic/types-septic-systems). Knowing the categories before that evaluation helps you understand the recommendation instead of just paying for whatever's specified.

## What counts as an alternative septic system?

Any system that adds treatment or dispersal beyond a standard septic tank and gravel or chamber drain field. The EPA's own list separates conventional systems (septic tank, gravel/stone drainfield, chamber system) from alternative systems, which includes drip distribution, aerobic treatment units, mound systems, recirculating sand filters, evapotranspiration systems, constructed wetlands, and cluster or community systems.

## What are the main types, and when is each one used?

**Mound systems** work in areas of shallow soil depth, high groundwater, or shallow bedrock. Effluent is pumped from the tank to a constructed sand mound in timed doses, where it's treated as it filters through the sand and disperses into the native soil below. Mounds need a lot of surface space and periodic maintenance, which makes them a common choice on rural lots with room to spare but poor natural soil.

**Aerobic treatment units (ATUs)** inject oxygen into the treatment tank, which increases bacterial activity and provides a higher level of treatment than an anaerobic conventional tank, similar in principle to a small-scale version of a municipal treatment plant. ATUs suit smaller lots, inadequate soil, high water tables, or homes near a surface water body sensitive to nutrient contamination. They require ongoing mechanical maintenance since they depend on pumps and aeration equipment rather than passive settling.

**Recirculating sand filters** pump effluent through a sand-filled bed (PVC-lined or concrete) under low pressure before the treated water reaches the drain field. They provide a high level of nutrient treatment and suit sites with high water tables or proximity to sensitive water bodies, at a higher cost than a conventional system.

**Drip distribution systems** insert drip laterals into the top 6 to 12 inches of soil rather than requiring a large mound. Their main advantage is a smaller physical footprint; the tradeoff is a required dose tank and electrical components for timed delivery, adding cost and maintenance a gravity system doesn't need.

**Evapotranspiration systems** use a watertight-lined drainfield where effluent evaporates into the air rather than filtering into soil, so it never reaches groundwater. These only work in arid climates with strong sun and heat, and they're vulnerable to failure if rainfall or snow exceeds what the system can evaporate off.

**Constructed wetlands** route wastewater through an engineered wetland cell (gravel, sand, and wetland plants) that mimics natural wetland treatment before the water either exits to a drainfield or disperses further.

**Cluster or community systems** serve two or more homes or buildings under shared ownership, common in rural subdivisions where individual lots are too small or unsuitable for separate systems.

## Why would a site need an alternative system instead of a conventional one?

Four site conditions typically force the decision: insufficient soil depth above bedrock or a restrictive layer, a high seasonal water table, a lot too small or too steeply sloped for a conventional field, or proximity to a lake, stream, or other water body sensitive to nutrient loading. A licensed site evaluator determines which of these conditions applies to a specific lot, and the type of alternative system prescribed follows directly from which limitation is present; a shallow-soil site typically points to a mound, while a nutrient-sensitive site near water often points to an ATU or sand filter.

## Do alternative systems need more maintenance than conventional ones?

Yes, generally. Any system with a pump, blower, timer, or electrical component needs regular mechanical maintenance and, in most states, more frequent professional inspection than a passive gravity system. The EPA notes that ATUs specifically should be expected to receive regular life-time maintenance, and most states require a service contract or annual inspection for advanced treatment systems as a condition of the permit. Budget for that ongoing cost, not just the installation price, when comparing an alternative system to a conventional one.

## Are alternative systems as reliable as conventional systems?

When properly designed, installed, and maintained for the site conditions they're specified for, yes. An alternative system isn't a downgrade; it's the correct engineering response to a site a conventional system genuinely can't serve. The reliability risk comes from skipped maintenance on the mechanical components, not from the underlying technology, which is why a service contract matters more for these systems than it does for a conventional tank and field.

## FAQ

**What is the best alternative septic system?**
There isn't a single best option; the right type depends entirely on the site limitation a licensed evaluator identifies, whether that's shallow soil, high groundwater, small lot size, or proximity to sensitive water. A mound suits shallow soil; an ATU or sand filter suits nutrient-sensitive sites.

**How much more does an alternative septic system cost than a conventional one?**
Expect roughly double to triple the cost of a conventional system, which runs $3,000 to $7,000 installed, compared to $7,000 to $20,000 or more for most alternative types. The gap reflects added pumps, sand media, or engineered treatment stages.

**Do alternative septic systems need a permit like conventional ones?**
Yes, and often a more detailed one, since the permitting authority needs to verify the system matches the specific site limitation it's designed to address. Expect a site evaluation, an engineered design, and in many states an ongoing maintenance or service contract as a permit condition.

**Can I install an alternative septic system myself?**
It's far less realistic than a conventional system due to the engineering, electrical, and mechanical components involved, and most states require licensed installation for these systems regardless of homeowner DIY allowances that may apply to simpler conventional setups. Check your state's specific rules before assuming otherwise.

**How long do alternative septic systems last?**
With proper maintenance, alternative systems can last as long as conventional systems, generally 20 to 30 years or more, though mechanical components like pumps and blowers typically need replacement on a shorter cycle within that lifespan.

## Bottom line

Alternative septic systems exist because some lots genuinely can't support a conventional drain field, not because they're a premium upgrade. The type you need follows from your site's specific limitation (soil depth, groundwater, space, or nearby water sensitivity), and the cost, generally $7,000 to $20,000 or more installed, reflects the added mechanical treatment that limitation requires. Get a licensed site evaluation before you assume which type applies to you.

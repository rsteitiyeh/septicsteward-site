A full drain field replacement costs $5,000 to $20,000 installed in 2026 for a conventional system, and $10,000 to $20,000 or more for an engineered alternative like a mound, according to current cost tracking from HomeGuide and Angi ([HomeGuide, 2026](https://homeguide.com/costs/drain-leach-field-replacement-cost); [Angi, 2026](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-new-drain-fields-foot.htm)). That range covers full replacement of a failed field, not the far cheaper repair of a localized problem, which typically runs $1,000 to $5,000 instead.

Replacement, not repair, becomes necessary when the field itself has failed (saturated soil, collapsed pipes, or biomat clogging across the whole field) rather than a single fixable issue like one crushed line or a clogged distribution box. Soil conditions, system type, and local permitting are what separate a $5,000 job from a $20,000 one.

## What actually drives the cost

Soil type is the single biggest variable: sandy, well-drained soil supports a straightforward conventional field at the lower end of the range, while clay, rock, or a high water table forces an engineered system (mound, chamber, or pressure-dosed design) that costs substantially more. Labor makes up a large share of the total regardless of soil, commonly estimated around 60% of the project cost, since a full replacement means excavation, new pipe and gravel or chamber installation, and backfilling, not just a targeted repair ([HomeGuide, 2026](https://homeguide.com/costs/drain-leach-field-replacement-cost)). A required perc test ($500 to $2,000) and local permits ($450 to $2,000) sit on top of the installation cost and are due before work starts, not included in most contractor quotes unless you ask.

## Conventional replacement vs. engineered replacement

A conventional gravity drain field, gravel and perforated pipe laid in trenches, is the cheaper option and works when soil drains adequately and the site is flat enough for gravity flow. When a perc test shows shallow bedrock, a high water table, or soil that's too slow or too fast draining, the health department will require an engineered alternative, most often a mound or a pressure-dosed chamber system, which adds a pump, a dosing tank, and more design work to the job. You won't know for certain which category your replacement falls into until the perc test is done, so treat the wide cost range as normal, not vague.

## When is it replacement, not repair

Repair covers a fixable, localized problem: a single crushed pipe, a clogged distribution box, or one damaged section of the field, and it typically runs $1,000 to $5,000. Full replacement is needed when the field has failed system-wide, standing water or sewage odor across the whole field, repeated backups even after pumping the tank, or a field that's simply reached the end of its service life (conventional fields commonly last 20 to 30 years with proper maintenance). A septic inspector can confirm which category you're in before you commit to a full replacement quote.

## How to budget realistically

Get at least two contractor quotes after your perc test results are in hand, since pricing swings widely by region and soil, and ask each quote to itemize the perc test, permit, excavation, materials, and pump (if an engineered system is required) separately. Set aside contingency for permit delays or an unexpected soil finding once excavation starts; rural counties in particular can add weeks to the permitting step. If your existing tank is also old or undersized, ask the contractor to inspect it during the same visit; replacing a field without addressing a failing tank just moves the next expensive call up.

## Bottom line

Budget $5,000 to $20,000 for a full drain field replacement depending on soil and system type, plus $1,000 to $4,000 combined for the perc test and permits before installation even starts. ## FAQ

**How long does a drain field replacement take from start to finish?**
Including the perc test, permit approval, and installation, a full replacement often takes four to eight weeks total, though installation itself is usually just a few days once permits are in hand. Permit delays, not the physical work, are the most common cause of a longer timeline.

**Can I replace just part of a drain field instead of the whole thing?**
Sometimes, if only one section has failed and the rest of the field tests as healthy, but many health departments require the full field to meet current code once you're doing permitted replacement work. Ask your inspector directly whether a partial replacement is allowed in your jurisdiction before budgeting for it.

**Does homeowners insurance cover drain field replacement?**
Standard homeowners insurance typically does not cover septic system failure from normal wear, age, or lack of maintenance, since that's treated as a maintenance issue rather than a covered peril. Some policies do cover sudden, accidental damage (a burst pipe from a covered event), so check your specific policy language before assuming it's out of pocket.

**How do I know if my drain field is actually failing versus just old?**
Standing water, sewage odor, or lush green grass in patches over the field, plus repeated backups even right after the tank is pumped, are signs of failure rather than just age. A septic inspection can distinguish between an aging-but-functional field and one that actually needs replacement.

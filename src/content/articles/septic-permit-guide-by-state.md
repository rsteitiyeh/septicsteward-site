Nearly every state requires a permit before you install, repair, replace, or significantly alter a septic system, and in most states that permit is issued by the county or local health department rather than a single state agency. The exact application, fee, and site evaluation requirements vary by state and even by county, but the underlying process is consistent nationwide: submit a site evaluation, get plans approved, pass inspection, then get final sign-off.

The variation homeowners run into isn't usually about whether a permit is required (it almost always is) but about who issues it, what the site evaluation covers, and how much it costs. A handful of states run permitting through a single environmental agency; most delegate it to county health departments, which means two counties in the same state can have different fees and timelines for what looks like an identical project.

## Do you need a permit to install or repair a septic system?

Yes, in nearly every state, for new installation and for most repairs beyond minor emergency fixes. Texas, for example, requires a permit and approved plan to construct, alter, extend, or repair an on-site sewage facility, according to the [Texas Commission on Environmental Quality](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/ossf/ossfpermits.html), with a narrow exemption for large rural lots (10 acres or more) that meet specific site and setback conditions. West Virginia similarly requires a permit from the local county health department before any installation, repair, or modification, with the process designed specifically to confirm a new or altered system matches the site's soil and groundwater conditions.

Minor emergency repairs are the common exception. Texas rules, for instance, exempt emergency fixes like replacing a tank lid or repairing a riser cap from advance permitting, but still require the repair to be reported to the permitting authority in writing, typically within 72 hours.

## Who issues the permit: state or county?

It depends on the state, and this is the detail that trips up the most homeowners. In South Carolina, permits and plan approval come from the state's Department of Environmental Services, and [without that approval, the county won't issue a building permit for the home itself](https://des.sc.gov/permits-regulations/septic-tanks/permits-licenses/septic-tanks-residential-single-home-builder), tying the septic permit directly to construction approval. Florida runs a hybrid model in transition: permits in most counties are issued by the county health department under Florida Department of Health oversight, while a subset of counties has shifted to direct Florida Department of Environmental Protection permitting as part of an ongoing multi-year transfer.

Washington State regulates septic systems under a statewide administrative code, but [local health departments frequently apply stricter rules on top of the state baseline](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/wastewater-management/septic-system), so the state minimum isn't always the number that governs your specific project. Always confirm with the county, not just the state agency, before assuming you know the requirement.

## What does the permit process usually involve?

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Site evaluation | Soil type, depth to bedrock, water table depth, slope assessed by a licensed evaluator or the health department |
| 2. Application and site plan | Submitted with property survey, floor plan, and evaluation results |
| 3. Plan review | Permitting authority reviews for compliance with state and local code |
| 4. Permit issuance | Approved plan and fee paid before construction begins |
| 5. Construction inspection | Installer's work checked against approved plan before backfilling |
| 6. Final approval | System signed off as complete and compliant |

Texas rules require the permitting authority to approve or deny an application within 30 days of receipt, giving homeowners a concrete timeline expectation, though actual turnaround often depends on how backed up the local office is.

## How much does a septic permit cost?

Permit fees typically run $50 to $500 or more depending on the state and county, with the fee itself only one part of total permitting cost once you add the required site evaluation and design plans. Florida county health department fees for a standard application commonly fall in the $475 to $525 range, though the exact number depends on the county and system type. As one documented example, a homeowner in Wake County, North Carolina paid $325 for a perc test, $750 for design drawings, and $275 for the county permit itself, a combined $1,350 in pre-construction costs before installation began, illustrating how the permit fee is usually the smallest line item in the total.

Because these figures shift by county and change over time, confirm the current fee schedule directly with your local health department or permitting authority before budgeting a project.

## Does a septic system need to be inspected as part of permitting, or after?

Both. A site evaluation happens before the permit is issued to confirm the property can support the proposed system, and a construction inspection happens after installation but before the system is backfilled, to confirm it was built to the approved plan. Some states add a third layer: a separate maintenance inspection requirement on a recurring schedule after the system is in use, particularly for advanced or alternative systems with mechanical components.

## What happens if you skip the permit?

You risk having to remove and reinstall the system to code, facing fines, and in states like South Carolina, being unable to get a building permit for the home itself since septic and construction approval are directly linked. An unpermitted system can also complicate or block a future home sale, since a transfer inspection or title search can surface the missing permit record even years later.

## FAQ

**Do you need a permit to install a septic tank?**
Yes, in nearly every state and county, for both new installations and most repairs beyond minor emergency fixes. The permitting authority is usually the county health department, though a handful of states run the process through a state environmental agency instead.

**How much does a septic tank permit cost?**
Typically $50 to $500 for the permit itself, though total pre-construction cost, once you add the required site evaluation and design plans, often runs $1,000 to $1,500 or more. Costs vary significantly by state and county.

**Who do I contact for septic permit requirements in my state?**
Start with your county health department or local environmental health office, since most states delegate permitting to the county level even when a state agency sets the baseline rules. Your state's environmental protection or health department website usually links to the correct local office.

**How long does it take to get a septic permit?**
Turnaround varies by state and how backed up the local office is, though some states set a maximum review window; Texas, for example, requires approval or denial within 30 days of a complete application.

**Can I install my own septic system without a licensed installer?**
Some states allow homeowner installation on a home you own and occupy yourself, provided you meet all permitting, construction, and maintenance requirements, but this varies significantly by state and system type, and alternative systems with mechanical components are much less likely to qualify. Confirm directly with your permitting authority before assuming DIY installation is allowed.

## Bottom line

A permit is required in nearly every state before you install, repair, or replace a septic system, issued most often by the county health department rather than a single state office. The process (site evaluation, application, plan review, construction inspection, final approval) is consistent nationwide even though fees and exact requirements shift by state and county. Confirm the current rule with your local health department before you budget or schedule work, since the number you find online may already be out of date.

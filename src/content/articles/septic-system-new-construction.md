Building on land without city sewer means the septic system has to be designed and permitted before you pour a foundation. That starts with a soil evaluation (a perc test) done by the local health department, followed by a design, a permit, tank and drain field placement, and an inspection before the system goes in the ground. Skipping any step can delay your building permit or force an expensive redesign later.

The specifics (tank size, setback distances, allowed system type) come from your county or state health code, not a national standard. The soil and lot layout you're building on determine most of the rest.

## What is a perc test and why do you need one first

A percolation test measures how fast water drains through your soil, and it's the first thing a health department wants before approving any septic design. A technician digs a test hole, fills it with water, and times the drop rate; slow-draining clay soil needs a larger or engineered drain field, while sandy soil often supports a standard system. Most states require this test (or an equivalent soil evaluation) before issuing a septic permit, and results are typically valid for a set number of years. Expect to pay for the test regardless of what type of system you end up needing.

## How is the tank sized for a new home

Tank size is set by bedroom count, not square footage, because bedrooms are used as a proxy for expected daily wastewater flow. Most state health codes size a standard single-family tank starting around 1,000 gallons for a two- or three-bedroom home, stepping up to roughly 1,250 gallons for four bedrooms and 1,500 gallons for five, though your local code is the final word and some counties round up for safety margin. If you're finishing a basement with a future bedroom or bathroom, size up now; retrofitting a tank after the yard is landscaped costs far more than building it in.

| Bedrooms | Typical minimum tank size | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 2 to 3 | 1,000 gallons | Common state code minimum |
| 4 | 1,250 gallons | Some counties require 1,500 |
| 5 | 1,500 gallons | Verify with local health dept. |
| 6+ | Engineered design | Site-specific sizing required |

## Where can the tank and drain field go

Setback distances from wells, property lines, water bodies, and the house are set by your state or county code, and they're non-negotiable on the permit application. A common baseline many states use is roughly 50 to 100 feet from any well and at least 10 feet from the foundation and property lines, but some jurisdictions require more near lakes, streams, or shallow groundwater. The EPA notes that local health departments run the site assessment and set these distances specifically to protect groundwater and drinking water sources, so plan your driveway, garage, and landscaping around the approved drain field footprint rather than the other way around ([EPA septic systems guidance](https://www.epa.gov/septic)).

## When should the system go in relative to the house

Install the tank and drain field before the driveway is paved and before heavy landscaping equipment starts working the yard. Soil compaction from trucks and construction equipment driving over an unmarked drain field area is one of the most common causes of early system failure, and it's avoidable if the area is flagged and fenced off from day one. Coordinate with your builder so the septic contractor's excavation happens on the same rough schedule as the foundation dig, not as an afterthought once the house is framed.

## What does a new septic system cost in 2026

A conventional gravity-fed system for an average single-family home typically runs $3,000 to $10,000 installed, according to 2026 cost data from Angi, while engineered systems required by difficult soil (mound, aerobic treatment, or pressure-dosed designs) run notably higher ([Angi, 2026](https://www.angi.com/articles/what-does-it-cost-install-septic-system.htm)). Separate 2026 industry cost tracking from SepticTankHub puts engineered mound systems specifically at $10,000 to $25,000 installed, which lines up with the Angi range for non-conventional soil conditions ([SepticTankHub, 2026](https://www.septictankhub.com/blog/mound-septic-systems/)). Add the perc test, design/engineering fee, and permit fee on top; those typically run a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars combined and are due before installation even starts.

- Perc test and soil evaluation: usually a few hundred dollars, paid to a licensed evaluator
- Design and engineering: required for non-standard soil, priced per site
- Permit fee: set by the county health department
- Conventional tank and drain field installed: $3,000 to $10,000 (2026 average)
- Engineered system (mound, ATU, pressure dose) installed: $10,000 to $25,000+ (2026 average)

## Bottom line

Line up the perc test and health department permit before you finalize the house plans, size the tank for the bedroom count you'll actually have (including future finished space), and keep equipment off the drain field footprint once it's staked. ## FAQ

**Do I need a septic permit before I can get a building permit?**
In most counties, yes: the health department has to approve the septic design and issue a permit before the county will issue the building permit for the house itself. Check with your local building and health departments early, since this step often sets your whole construction timeline.

**How long does the septic permitting process take?**
It varies by county, but budget four to eight weeks from perc test to approved permit in a typical jurisdiction, longer if your soil requires an engineered design. Rural counties with a single part-time health inspector can take longer during busy building seasons.

**Can I install a septic system myself on new construction?**
Most states require a licensed septic installer for new systems, and self-installed systems on new construction often fail the final health department inspection. Even where DIY is technically allowed, lenders and insurers frequently require professional installation and a certified inspection before closing.

**What happens if the perc test fails?**
A failed perc test doesn't mean you can't build; it usually means you need an engineered system (mound, aerobic treatment unit, or pressure-dosed drain field) instead of a conventional gravity system, which raises the cost but is still a permitted path in nearly every jurisdiction.
